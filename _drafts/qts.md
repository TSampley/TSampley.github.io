As a member of the LGBTQIA+ community, I'm exhausted by typing (let alone saying) that phrase anytime I need to refer to myself or our community. More importantly, I've come to consider it ironically exclusionary. In an attempt to be more representative, we've ended up deciding who deserves full or primary representation and who only deserves partial or implied representation.
For those new to or outside the community, the extended acronym stands for (L)esbian, (G)ay, (B)isexual, (T)rans, (Q)ueer, (I)ntersex, (A)sexual, and more (+). Some people also use the Q to refer to "questioning": someone exploring their identity.
You can read a brief history of the terms' and acronym's evolution at National Geographic: https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/from-lgbt-to-lgbtqia-the-evolving-recognition-of-identity
You might notice a little bit of redundancy - Lesbian Gay and Bisexual all fall under Queer. It's easy for me to understand why it evolved this way: when tackling any concept, humans focus on the largest differences first, and we as we wrap our heads around those more granular ideas, we're able to slowly refine our understanding and develop greater nuance. LG and B were used originally, but over time members argued that it didn't fully represent everyone who felt a part of the community: those who fell outside the accepted norms of gender and sexuality. We eventually came to the point that we felt the need to tack on "Queer" to represent those who didn't fall under the granular lesbian, gay, or bisexual while feeling the need to maintain the historical LGB. Subsequently, some also add "I" for intersex and "A" for asexual, but it seems we've hit a complexity ceiling as even the most ardent allies simply add a plus to acknowledge that even the extended acronym doesn't cover everyone. So why do we still give primary recognition to LGB? I do think it's important to represent intersex, asexual, and aromantic persons, but I would argue they fall under queer and trans.
I think it's time we drop the original letters "LGB" in favor of a simplified, and therefor more representative, QT. In encompasses the essence of the movement in my mind. If you're straight and cis, you're already supported by the status quo. The whole reason these communities were started was to provide support and validation for those of us who exist outside the norm. If you're not straight and sexual, you're queer. If you're not cis and express yourself in accepted ways, you're trans. By distilling the acronym down to these two dimensions of identity, we allow everyone, regardless of historical granularity, to stand on equal footing.

---

I know that we do still have discrimination issues within our communities with some members accusing bisexuals of being gays-in-transition or asexuals of being trauma victims amongst many other harmful stereotypes, but it's my hope that we can move past these misperceptions together, recognizing that we all exist outside the norm, and supporting each other as a community of QTs.
