The most popular interpretation of human laughter that I'm aware of is the "subversion of expectations" model. But I'm under the impression this doesn't capture the general phenomenon of laughter, which is found across species. Instead, we should be framing it as a "resolution of tension*".
Imagine that you're a little kid again and you think you're about to go out for ice cream, but then &lt;something bad happens&gt; and you're not going out anymore. Your expectations have been subverted, but it's far from funny. This model works for comedy, where it was formulated, but fails to explain many other instances of laughter.

---

*tension - a bit informal, but this is meant to represent any perception of a threat either immediate or future.
Play Fighting
If we look at rodents, they seem to be ticklish, emitting high-pitched squeaks when tickled by researchers or even when play fighting together. But why do we laugh when we're tickled? Let's think about what's consistent between all those ticklish spots. Typical ticklish spots include the arm pits, ribs and sides, the neck, and the bottoms of our feet. The first three all have extremely vital structures immediately beneath the skin, making them very vulnerable; while we may not be likely to bleed out through the feet easily, as bipedal organisms, a foot injury can be debilitating and just as deadly. Tickle fights are a form of play that encourage us to practice protecting those vital spots in a low-risk way. Play is a large enough topic on its own, but the takeaway is that play encourages behaviors that prepare us for real threats in a low-risk way. We are engaging in interactions that may normally be threatening, like being chased in tag or concealing yourself in hide-and-seek, and using laughter to indicate we don't actually feel threatened, which also releases endorphins encouraging more play.
Bullying
Not to get depressing, but this does also explain why bullies and masochists laugh at the suffering of others. In short, it indicates that your suffering is not a problem to them. Someone who cares about you considers your suffering their own - a threat to you is a threat to them - but since your pain is inconsequential to a bully, laughing indicates the lack of tension they feel from your suffering.
Back to Comedy
So how does this explain comedy and jokes? Where's the threat - the tension? The "subversion of expectations" model is easy to observe here: the setup establishes expectations and the punchline subverts them. And the resolution of tension framing? We need to shift our focus entirely to the punchline.
The setup is still important, because it does establish particular expectations, but the critical part is the punchline creating a brief moment of cognitive dissonance - which can trigger the same response in the brain as a physical threat - immediately resolved by the recognition of the subversive meaning, resolving the feeling of cognitive dissonance, triggering our reflex to indicate "s'all good" - thus we laugh.
This model definitely needs to be better articulated, because in some cases the laughter is a response to relieving tension and in others it's used to relieve tension, reversing the causal relationship, but always strongly associated. Which suggests to me that these two are unified by seeing laughter as one of the first forms of language. In all cases, it's clear that a lack of tension is being communicated, before or after the resolution.
I feel that this model definitely covers more cases, but if you have a counter-example that you believe defies this model, I would love to hear it. Thanks for reading! (and apologies for no sources at this time - I just wanted to get the idea out)
