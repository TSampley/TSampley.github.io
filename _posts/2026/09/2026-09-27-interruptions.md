As someone with ADHD, let me start by saying: ADHD is not an excuse to interrupt someone.

{% include image.html url="/assets/img/headers/interrupters.jpg" alt="" caption="" %}

Interruptions are dehumanizing. The entire reason that humans and other species develop communication – let alone language – is to transfer information between agents.
When someone decides to speak, it's because they're attempting to transfer information for some purpose.
When another agent begins speaking while the first is still speaking, it's because they have decided for themselves that the first doesn't need to finish whatever they're saying, and it's far more important for the second to speak.
Either the second believes that they can anticipate exactly what the first is going to or trying to say, or they believe that whatever the first is saying isn't important. It is the embodiment of arrogance to assume you know what is in someone's mind and what they're trying to convey and to decide for all parties that it's more important for you to make noise.
There are times that it is appropriate to interrupt, but it's usually only when someone is making statements about you specifically that you know to be false or malicious that it's acceptable and even expected.
By far the majority of cases are not acceptable, but it still happens frequently for a variety of reasons.

Some people feel the need to validate themselves by sharing all the information they know about some given subject matter. Some people are so insecure that they even make up information in an attempt to feel smarter. I had the extreme displeasure of living with one of these creatures for a while – this was the same creature that had tried to recruit me to commit a community shooting in Stillwater. The same creature that was diagnosed with autism by its school and never completed its sophomore year because its parents took it out for homeschooling which they never actually provided.

I was trying to tell it about a time I was living at the local homeless shelter when one of the staff started harassing me about not having family and then decided to call the police and lie that I said I wanted to kill their staff, so I got involuntarily admitted. I went on to tell the thing about my conversation with the therapist about manufactured consent – even referring to the book by Noam Chomsky and giving her the publishing date - and the thing interrupted me all excited trying to say it understood exactly what I meant about how the homeless shelter worker lied and worked with the police to get me admitted, and I had to jump in and say "no, that's not what manufacturing consent means..." but they went on to try to defend their interpretation of my story as an example of manufactured consent, because they schizophrenically assumed the conversation with the therapist was directly related to how I got there. I tried to stop the thing several times, but it just kept going on, excited that it "understood" something and it couldn't stop itself from explaining its interpretation because it was just so happy to have pulled a massive turd nugget out of its saggy ass.

I complained to their right-hand-man about it, and they actually tried to defend it by saying "bUt sHe HaS AdhD" as if that somehow made it acceptable. But this was the same person that tried to convince me that 2+2=5 by something they called "industrial math", which I tried to explain to them was not true, valid math, to which they replied "no really! look it up!". Stupid loves stupid company, because it doesn't feel threatened.

Though, I would say the majority of cases with rational humans occur when the listener has something prepared that they want to say and the speaker pauses briefly, either for a breath or at the end of a sentence, which the eager listener uses as their chance to jump-in. From their perspective, they're not interrupting at all, but that's only because their attention shifted to what's on their mind instead of the rest of the speaker's sentence – they register the pause but not the meaning of the final words.

## Information Flows

More important than any number of bad examples of communication is the ideal we should all be trying to follow.

Again, I find interruptions dehumanizing; however, I will be the first to say that some times you have to interrupt someone when they're rambling on and on, which I am guilty of myself. When I'm rambling and someone interrupts, it doesn't bother me – it needs to be done. I'm trying to get better about breaking down complex ideas into smaller digestable parts for people unfamiliar with the things I talk about, but some ideas are just inherently complex and require a sentence that lasts a full 10 seconds.

The challenge is that productive communication requires all parties to have sufficient humility to know when it's **not** their turn to talk. The larger the group, the more difficult it is. When the conversation is between just two or three parties, and one person can't stop interrupting or railroading the whole conversation, that's simply a failure of the individual – usually driven by a need to feel valuable.

Of course, I'm far from the first to notice these problems and try to solve them in some way to guarantee productive communication. Even "professionals" at the highest levels of government need rules to engage and a dedicated position to referee their interactions (see the Speaker of the House), which just highlights for me how many of our representatives are still mentally children.

Ironically, it's easiest to implement the ideal with AIs, but if any organics are interested, the key, as I've already mentioned, is humility. There are a few concrete points below for each role.

### As a Speaker
- Try to speak concisely – don't take up too much time and dominate the conversation; a conversation with one speaker is a lecture.
- Don't try to simplify your message in anticipation of the audience not understanding; if they don't understand, they can ask a question.
- Qualify your statements adequately and don't overstate your certainty (which is a whole other discussion).

### As a Listener
- Listen **actively**: respond **facially** so the speaker has active feedback and can pause if you need to ask a question or clarify something before they move on.
- If you have a thought or question while someone is speaking (especially in larger groups), raise your hand, finger or give other indication that you would like to interject.
- When someone presents an idea or information that conflicts with your worldview, ask a question about the specific contradictory element(s) of the idea.

Listening is just as much a part of a conversation as speaking. If you're eager to share and participate, try not to interrupt, and you stand a much better chance of participating in a way that's actually relevant, rather than detracting from the conversation because you pathologically need the spotlight on yourself at all times.
